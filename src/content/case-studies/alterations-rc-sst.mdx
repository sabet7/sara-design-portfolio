###### Overview
## An analog system causing bottlenecks and mishaps throughout employee daily workflows. 
{/* Alterations by R.C.’s analog system created miscommunication and organizational issues that affected the employee’s daily workflows and ultimately customers. */} 
A small garment business was growing, which meant more incoming orders. However, it's current system could not keep up with the growing demand. Employees and clients faced unnecessary friction, bottlenecks and delays. Backed-up, delayed, or misplaced orders began to affect the store’s reputation and  newfound momentum. Here's how I rebuilt that system,and what happened when it got tested by conditions we didn't plan for.

##### SOLUTION
## A self-service kiosk working in tandem with a work management system 
{/* An all in one work management order system that works with a customer facing kiosk to streamline daily operations and provides sales data so that the owner can monitor business growth. */} 
Alteration’s new system is a unified platform that handles front-end customer service interactions and daily operations and management. The process begins with the customer-facing kiosk. A customer drops off their garment, generating a unique garment ticket. 

After the ticket enters the system, it gets organized by pick up date, prioritizing earlier picks on the operation pipeline to ensure timely completion of orders. After the tailor finishes his or her job, the ticket is marked off for review by the head tailor or seamstress, and only get marked as done after approval.  

On the business management side, completed orders translate into sales. The business owner can track his sales by month or quarter, seeing where he could improve marketing or different areas of his buiness. 

###### Discovery
## Backed-up orders started to get in the way of business.
While I was working on Alterations website, I worked at the store for two weeks. During those two weeks, I noticed that it's growth was outpacing it's current operating system. 

At the time, the store relied heavily on ticket stubs because that is how it has always been done. However, the heavy reliance on paper tickets caused back ups and mishaps. 

Additionally, there was a small staff who were being stretched thin.  

![Service blueprint of operations](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/alterations-service-blueprint.png)

Based on my observations, I created a service blueprint to see areas where front facing operations and backend operations could improve. Employees had to take on many tasks, thus limiting their time to work on garments and the ticket stubs were unreliable. 

##### Pain Points
- Cognitive overload: The tailors occupied two roles at once, tailors and sales associates. 
- Unstructured legacy system: Additionally, the system in place was outdated and relied heavily on analog artifacts, which did not leave room for automation or organization.‍ 

Key Insight: Daily operations heavily relied on data provided by the customer; however, the current system did not utilize this information well. ‍

###### Research
##### Competitive analysis
![Competitive analysis](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/alterations-competitive-a.png)
Although there are many business management and work management software available, they were incompatible for a tailoring business. They were best suited for projects and IT related tasks, not for handling or managing garments. 

After more research,  I came across Geelus, a software  specifically for tailoring and dry cleaning businesses. 

![Geelus UI is too complicated for employees](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/geelus-2.png)

However, the interface of this softwares was better suited for dry cleaning businesses. Additionally, it did now prioritize tickets, which was necessary to ease the workflow of employees at Alterations by R.C. 

###### Process
## How might I bridge customer facing experiences and daily operations into a streamlined system while maintaining human interactions?

<VoiceNote src="/media/case-studies/alterations-by-rc-sst/content/voice-note-alterations-process.mp3" label="Maintaining human connections in the design"/>

I needed to create a system that would ease the workload of tailors but also keep personal interactions between customers and staff. By talking to the business owners and staff, I came to understand that this system need to mimics a mental model that tailors are used to. 

On the customer-facing side, I wanted to make sure that human interaction was kept. 

The first access point for this system was the self-service kiosk. The kiosk would serve as an aid to tailors who might be busy doing fittings or working on large orders. The self-service kiosk would handle simple interactions— drop offs for adding buttons or zippers, scheduling fittings, or picking up items. Thus, eliminating customer wait times and work for tailors. 

![Workflow of operations](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/alterations-workflow.png)

###### Engineering + Design
## The engineering side of designing this system
### Linking the self-service kiosk to operations.
The kiosk now allowed for customer pick-ups, another task that tailors needed to attend to. Creating the pick-up flow was tricky.  How can I create something that keeps a history of picked-up items for the business owner, but clears out every week? 

After asking the tailors and owner a few questions and bouncing a ideas around in Claude, figured that a clean array every week would work. It was a risky move, but it made the new digitized work order system feasible to manage given that there was no compliance or data officers among the staff. 

<VoiceNote src="/media/case-studies/alterations-by-rc-sst/content/voice-note-alterations-engineering-process.mp3" label="On engineering"/>

### Co-creating the MVP with Claude and publishing on Vercel to stress test new system 
In order to test the effectiveness of my design, I wanted to create a working prototype (well, a bit more than a prototype) that employees and customers could use. This MVP was to see how this new system would impact business operations and test customer use in real time. 

### Technical limitations and considerations
One technical limitation was connecting the current payment system to the kiosk.  Clients pay via a Verifone. The receipt issued after payment was typically attached to the the garment ticket. However, it was not always a reliable way to confirm payments. I needed to connect the current system to the device in store and ensure that payments made were reflected in the system.

After designing the main screens, I hopped on Github and set up my code space and set up a staging layer in Supabase with SQL tables that would store login credentials for employees, and ticket information for the garments.

![Supabase workflow](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/supabase-set-up.png)

However, in desiging and building this system, one question persisted: how would I handle data storage? Not only was I introducing a new system to how the tailors worked but I also had to consider the data system working behind the scenes. 

###### Current designs
To reduce employee’s cognitive load, I designed a dashboard that kept track of all the tickets and prioritized them according to pick up dates. The sections on the dashboards were inspired by "In" and "Out" trays, creating a clear and understandable hierarchy.

![Employee dashboards](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/alterations-sst-employee-dashboards.png)

### Self service kiosk
On the customer facing side, customers could easily pick up and drop off items, without interrupting the employee’s workflow.

## Visually tying it all together.
Although the experiences for the frontend and backend were different, they needed to feel part of the same business. I wanted to achieve this through a visual guideline. It wasn’t just for aesthetics, but rather to inform future design. 

![Visual identity](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/alterations-sst-visuals.png)

### Roadblocks during MVP stage
A few days before testing the MVP design, I learned that the shop no longer had WiFi connection. The new paper system was created, not as an afterthought, but as conscious fallback system for when things don't work. 

![MVP stage](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/mvp-stage-1.png)

With these new constraints, I had to pivot and test the system in a different way. I chose to test out an analog version of my design to see if the new organizational architecture would assist the employees and create a mental model for when the new digitized system would be implemented.

![Store setup](/media/case-studies/alterations-rc-sst/content/store-setup.png)

The re-design consisted of a manual for daily operations, a new receipts booklet that allowed the sales associate to detail the type of alteration the garment needed, color code stickers, a big binder and visual identity guideline.

The analog MVP has worked and held on so far. Once the WiFi issues are resolved, the owners are eager to implement the digital system I created.

###### Reflection
## UIs doesn't only live in digital spaces.
<VoiceNote src="/media/case-studies/alterations-by-rc-sst/content/voice-note-alterations-learnings.mp3"label="On what I learned in the process"/>
As a designer, I've always believed that user interfaces exist on digital surfaces and that designing for digital experiences is sole job of a UX or product designer.  However, when I had to adapt my design and revert back to an analog format, I realized that it was still design. I was still creating an experience informed by user needs and feedback that was aimed at solving a specific problem.
